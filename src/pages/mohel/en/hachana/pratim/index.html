Why I ask about each detail

  • Day and time of birth. These tell me when the eighth day falls, especially when the birth was close to sunset. The time in the letter isn't always accurate to the minute, so I'll ask what you remember.
  • Type of birth. A vaginal birth, a C-section or an assisted delivery affect the preparations and the timing, so it's good for me to know.
  • Week of birth. A baby born early gets extra attention, and the doctor and the mohel coordinate with each other.
  • Weight and feeding. This shows me how the baby is getting stronger. If he eats very little and sleeps a great deal, I want to know about it.
  • Jaundice. This is one of the first things I ask about. I make the decision about the brit date together with the doctor – not the parents, and not me alone.

The hospital discharge letter: what it says and where to find each detail

When you go home from the hospital, you get a discharge letter. Here is what I look for in it, and it helps if you know where to find each item:

  • Week of birth. Usually at the top of the letter, next to the birth details.
  • Apgar score. Two numbers between 0 and 10, measured twice in the first minutes. They describe how the baby responded at birth.
  • Birth weight and discharge weight. These give the percentage of weight lost. Some loss in the first days is normal; the weight levels off around day four and gets back to birth weight after about ten days.
  • Bilirubin, or jaundice. The letter shows a test result and a recommendation for a repeat test at your health fund (kupat cholim). Don't interpret the result yourself – send it to me and we'll decide together with the doctor.
  • Time of birth and special notes. Anything the doctors wrote specifically, even if it seems small to you.

Easiest of all: take a photo of the letter and send it to me on WhatsApp. I know how to read it, even when it's in Hebrew.

My check before the brit

Before the brit I examine the baby: I look at the eyes and skin color to see if there is jaundice, at his liveliness (a strong cry and movement) and at the area itself, and I ask about feeding and sleep. If he eats very little and sleeps a great deal, tell me.

It's best to schedule this check about two days before the brit. That way, if there is jaundice, it can be tested again to see which way it's going. When needed, I also come to your home.

When the brit milah takes place: the eighth day

The brit milah is held on the eighth day after birth. If the baby was born before sunset, the day of birth counts as day one. If he was born after sunset, you start counting from the next day. The brit is done only when the baby is healthy and fit for it, which is why jaundice is checked beforehand.

The time between sunset and nightfall is called bein hashmashot (twilight): it's uncertain whether it is still day or already night. So if the birth was close to sunset, send me the exact time and we'll check it together with the rabbi. A baby born by C-section has his brit on the eighth day as usual, but not on Shabbat, Yom Tov or Yom Kippur.

You can work out the date with the brit milah date calculator, and all the rules are in the chapter “The brit date and the jaundice check”.

Jaundice and the brit date

The baby has a jaundice test. Tell the mohel the result, along with the day and hour the test was done, because that matters. If the jaundice is borderline or high, you'll be asked to do more tests. Things can still change, and the brit is done only when the baby is fit for it. The baby's health comes first.

The mohel guides you through setting the date and makes sure your son is healthy enough for a brit on time. Sometimes even a healthy baby doesn't have his brit on the eighth day, so it's essential to talk with the mohel ahead of time.

What else to prepare for the day itself

  • Feeding. Feed the baby until about an hour before the brit. In the last hour you don't feed him, and until then let him eat as much as he likes.
  • A two-piece outfit. An undershirt or top, plus pants that come off easily. That way the baby isn't fully undressed and doesn't get cold.
  • A diaper bag. Cloth diapers, disposable diapers, wipes, a change of clothes, formula if he isn't breastfed, a pacifier and diaper cream.

Frequently asked questions

What does a newborn's hospital discharge letter include?

The week of birth, the Apgar score, birth weight and discharge weight, the bilirubin (jaundice) result and a follow-up recommendation, the time of birth and any special notes from the doctors.

Which details should I send the mohel before the brit milah?

The day and time of birth (especially if it was close to sunset), the type of birth, the week of birth, weight, how the baby is feeding, the jaundice status and anything special the doctors said.

How much weight does a baby lose in the first days?

A drop of up to about 7% in the first days is usually normal, and then the weight goes up again. Tipat Chalav and the doctor follow it.

Why does the exact time of birth matter?

It decides which day counts as day one. Born before sunset – the day of birth is day one; after sunset – you count from the next day. If the birth was close to sunset, send the exact time and we'll check it together with the rabbi.

My baby has jaundice. Is the brit postponed?

It depends on the jaundice level and its trend. Send me the test results with the day and hour they were taken. If needed, the brit is postponed according to the mohel's instructions until the baby is completely ready.

The baby was born by C-section. What changes?

The brit is on the eighth day as usual, but not on Shabbat, Yom Tov or Yom Kippur. If the eighth day falls on one of them, the brit is held on the first day after it that isn't Shabbat or a festival.

Does the form save my details?

No. The form is free with no sign-up, and the site doesn't save or send anything. The details appear only in the WhatsApp message that you send yourself.

Can I just send a photo of the discharge letter?

Yes, that's the simplest way. Send the photo on WhatsApp – I know how to read it, and if anything is missing we'll fill it in together.
