The first week

Shalom Zachor, Tachdid and Kvatter

Eight days between the birth and the brit, and within them a few beautiful customs. Here's what is customary and how it fits into the week's calendar. All according to your family's custom.

עבריתEnglishРусский

Shalom Zachor – the first Friday night

In many communities it's customary to hold a Shalom Zachor (also spelled Shalom Zachar) on the first Friday night after the birth.

In practice it's simple: after the Shabbat meal, anyone who wants comes to the house for light refreshments, to sing and to give blessings. If the mother and baby are still in the hospital, you can hold it at the parents' or grandparents' home. No invitations needed, no hall needed. Whoever knows, comes.

Brit Yitzchak and Tachdid – the evening before the brit

On the evening before the brit it's customary to hold a real event: family and friends gather for Torah study, reading the Zohar, singing, piyutim and refreshments – and pray for the baby's wellbeing (“Leil Shimurim”, the night of watching; in the Moroccan custom – “Tachdid”). I'd be glad to come to the Brit Yitzchak, lead the evening and give a short talk – arrange it with me in advance.

Kvatter – who carries the baby in

Bringing in the baby (“kvatter”) and placing him on the Chair of Elijah follow your family's custom. The kvatter carries the baby into the brit room and passes him from hand to hand. You can honor several. In many families this is the honor given to a couple waiting to have children.

It's customary for one close person to be the sandek, and another close person to read the brit blessings and the newborn's name. If you like, you can also ask the mohel to chant the blessings in the traditional melody and say the baby's name.

An important note

Make sure none of your relatives who may be expecting an honor feels hurt. Find out ahead of time in the family who traditionally gets to be sandek for the first son, and so on – and everyone will be happy.

Timeline of the first week

  • The day of birth: mazal tov! Write down the exact time of birth – it decides the day of the brit. Let the mohel know.
  • Days 2–3: jaundice check and discharge. Send the mohel the hospital discharge letter – it includes the jaundice results. Book the venue, catering and guests.
  • The first Friday night: Shalom Zachar (according to custom).
  • Days 5–7: a last check before the brit. Send the mohel a photo – he reviews it carefully, makes sure everything is ready and answers any question. On the evening before the brit – Brit Yitzchak.
  • The eighth day: the brit milah. In the daytime, before sunset. Feeding until about an hour before, a two-piece outfit, a diaper bag.

A mohel's tip

If you're not sure what your family's custom is, ask your parents and grandparents before you share out the honors. One conversation like that saves hurt feelings and brings the generations together around the baby.

How to count the eighth day

If the baby was born before sunset, the day of birth counts as day one. If he was born after sunset, day one is the following day. For example, a baby born on Monday before sunset has his brit the following Monday, if he is healthy and fit for it. A birth on Friday after sunset counts as a birth on Shabbat, so the brit is on the following Shabbat, if the baby is healthy. If the birth was during bein hashmashot or by C-section, the brit is not held on Shabbat and is set for Sunday.

The time between sunset and nightfall is called bein hashmashot (twilight). If the birth was then, or close to sunset, send me the exact time and we'll check it together with the rabbi. And Brit Yitzchak is always the evening before whatever day comes out.

Brit Yitzchak: what to prepare

A Brit Yitzchak doesn't need a big production. From the homes I visit, this is what you really need:

  • A place. Your home, your parents' home, or the synagogue. What matters is that the mother is comfortable – she has just given birth.
  • Light refreshments. Cakes, fruit, drinks. Not a meal.
  • Torah study. Who leads and what is read follow your family's custom. If you have no one, you can ask me.
  • A time. In the evening, usually after the Maariv prayer. Not too late – tomorrow is a big day.

Close family, friends, neighbors and people from the synagogue come. It doesn't have to be a big event: even ten people around a table with an open book and one song – that's a Brit Yitzchak. I bring a short talk and, if you like, songs and piyutim in your tradition. In between, it's the time for last-minute questions: what to bring tomorrow, when to feed, how to dress the baby.

Frequently asked questions

When is a Shalom Zachor held?

In many communities it's customary to hold a Shalom Zachor on the first Friday night after the birth.

What is a Tachdid?

That's the Moroccan name for the evening before the brit, the Brit Yitzchak: people gather for Torah study, reading the Zohar, singing, piyutim and refreshments, and pray for the baby's wellbeing.

Who can be the kvatter?

According to family custom. You can honor several, and in many families this is the honor for a couple waiting to have children.

Do you send invitations to a Shalom Zachor?

No. You don't need invitations or a hall: after the Shabbat meal, whoever knows comes for light refreshments, to sing and to give blessings.

The mother and baby are still in the hospital. Where do we hold the Shalom Zachor?

At the parents' or grandparents' home. What matters is getting together to sing and bless.

What's the difference between Brit Yitzchak, Leil Shimurim and Tachdid?

They are different names for the same evening before the brit. In all of them, family and friends gather for Torah study, the Zohar, singing and prayer for the baby's wellbeing.

Does the mohel come to the Brit Yitzchak?

I'd be glad to come to the Brit Yitzchak, lead the evening and give a short talk – arrange it with me in advance. It's also a good chance for me to see the baby the evening before.

How do I know which evening the Brit Yitzchak falls on?
