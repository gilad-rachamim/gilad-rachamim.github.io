Pidyon Haben – Redemption of the Firstborn

If this is the mother's first son, you may be obligated in the mitzvah of pidyon haben (redemption of the firstborn). The father redeems his son from a Kohen with five silver shekels, in a joyful ceremony with a festive meal.

The brit is behind you, the bandage is off, and the house is getting back to routine. Then, about three weeks after the brit, comes one more moment of joy: pidyon haben. Not every family is obligated, so first you check.

The details card I ask for after the birth has a line: "Mother's firstborn son? Yes / No". It is there exactly for this – so we know in advance whether a pidyon is coming. You can fill it in English too: the brit details form – free, no sign-up.

You are usually obligated when:

  • This is the first child born to the mother (even if the father has older children).
  • The birth was natural – not by C-section.
  • The father is not a Kohen or a Levi, and the mother is not the daughter of a Kohen or a Levi.
  • The mother had no earlier pregnancies that ended – otherwise ask a rabbi.

If even one of these points does not fit you, or you are not sure, it is a question for a rabbi. Don't decide on your own: one short question is better than a doubt.

When?

On the 31st day after the birth. If it falls on Shabbat or Yom Tov (a festival), it moves to the next day.

As with counting to the brit, everything starts from the day of birth and the time of birth relative to sunset. If you are not sure which day is day one, the chapter on the brit date explains it, and in any doubt you ask a rabbi.

Counting day 31 in practice

Write down the exact date and time of birth. In Judaism the day begins at sunset, so a baby born after sunset counts as born on the next day. From day one, count 31 days. If you land on Shabbat or a festival, the pidyon is the next day. The brit milah date calculator shows the sunset time on the day of birth and which day is day one.

Where it sits on the calendar

  • The eighth day: the brit milah. In the daytime, before sunset.
  • The day after the brit: a home visit. The mohel comes to your home and takes off the bandage with the care it needs – and keeps supporting you until full healing.
  • Day 31: pidyon haben (for a firstborn son). If you are obligated – the preparations are below.

What do you need?

  • A Kohen.
  • 5 silver coins (you can borrow them from a gemach, a free-loan fund).
  • A festive meal and a minyan.

Already at the meal after the brit, it is worth gently asking whether there is a Kohen in the family or the synagogue you could honor. That way, on day 31 you are not searching at the last minute.

Getting ready ahead of time

The Kohen

The easiest way to find a Kohen is through the family or the synagogue. Ask early, already at the brit, and agree on the day and time.

The coins

You need five silver coins. If you don't have them, you can borrow them from a gemach. Look into it a few days ahead, not on the morning of the ceremony.

Meal, minyan and invitation

Pidyon haben is held with a festive meal and a minyan. The invitation usually goes out about a week before day 31. How not to miss the date

Three weeks pass between the brit and day 31, and with a newborn at home the date slips away easily. That is why I ask about a firstborn already in the details card after the birth: if the answer is "yes", we know in advance that a pidyon haben is ahead, and there is time to prepare calmly.

If you are a mohel and keep a log of your brit ceremonies, the mohel's brit log lets you mark "mother's firstborn", and it shows the pidyon haben date – day 31 after the birth. That is free, no sign-up, as well.

For parents, the simplest way is to put day 31 in your phone calendar right after the brit, with a reminder a week before: that is exactly when it is time to send invitations, arrange the Kohen and find the coins.
