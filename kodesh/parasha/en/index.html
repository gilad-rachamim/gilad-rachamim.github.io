The weekly Torah portion, explained

Every Shabbat morning, in synagogues all over the world, a portion of the Torah is read aloud from a handwritten scroll. The Five Books of Moses are divided into 54 portions, called parshiot (one is a parsha, parashah or sidra), and they are read in order through the year, so that the whole Torah is completed every year. The portion for each week is known in advance from the Hebrew calendar, which is why the "parsha of the week" is printed in Jewish calendars, mentioned in the rabbi's sermon and studied at home before Shabbat.

How the portion of the week is found

This page calculates the portion from today's date, in your own browser. It finds the coming Shabbat, looks up its Hebrew date, and works out which portion falls on it in this Jewish year, including double portions and Shabbatot that fall on a holiday. Nothing needs to be updated by hand, and the page is right for any week of any year: choose a year in the calendar table to see all of its Shabbatot.

The name of each portion

Each portion is named after one of its first words. The first portion, Bereshit, is named after the first word of the Torah, "in the beginning"; Noach after Noah; Lech-Lecha after God's call to Abraham, "go forth". English spellings vary (Bereshit or Bereishit, Vayera or Vayeira), so the Hebrew name is shown beside each one.

The five books and their portions

Genesis (Bereshit) has 12 portions, Exodus (Shemot) 11, Leviticus (Vayikra) 10, Numbers (Bamidbar) 10 and Deuteronomy (Devarim) 11. The cycle begins after Simchat Torah, in October, with Genesis, and reaches Deuteronomy in the summer. The book of each week is shown under the portion's name, and the table of all 54 portions links every one to its full text.

Double portions and leap years

A regular Jewish year does not have enough free Shabbatot for 54 separate readings, so some neighboring portions are joined and read on the same Shabbat. The seven pairs that can be joined are Vayakhel-Pekudei, Tazria-Metzora, Achrei Mot-Kedoshim, Behar-Bechukotai, Chukat-Balak, Matot-Masei, Nitzavim-Vayeilech. A Jewish leap year adds a second month of Adar, about four more Shabbatot, and most of the pairs are read separately. The page joins them exactly as the calendar requires for each year.

Israel and the diaspora

Outside Israel some holidays last one more day. When that extra day, the last day of Pesach or the second day of Shavuot, falls on Shabbat, the diaspora reads the holiday portion that Shabbat, while Israel goes on to the next weekly portion. For a few weeks Israel is one portion ahead, until the diaspora reads a double portion and the two line up. Choose "Outside Israel" or "Israel" above; the choice is remembered on this device. Travelers usually follow the reading of the place where they are for Shabbat.

Holidays that replace the weekly reading

When a holiday such as Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot or Pesach falls on Shabbat, or on the Shabbat of the intermediate days, the regular portion is not read that week. Instead there is a special reading for the holiday, and the weekly portion moves to the next Shabbat. The tables mark these weeks as a holiday reading.

Your bar or bat mitzvah portion

A boy becomes bar mitzvah at 13 and a girl becomes bat mitzvah at 12, on the Hebrew birthday. Because the Hebrew and the civil calendars do not line up, the Hebrew birthday moves on the civil calendar every year. The calculator finds the Hebrew date of birth, the date of the bar or bat mitzvah, and the portion read on the first Shabbat on or after it, often the Shabbat on which the bar mitzvah boy is called to the Torah. Questions about which date to keep, especially for a birthday in Adar or on the 30th of a month, are for your rabbi.

Reading and studying the portion

Each portion is divided into seven sections, called aliyot, for the seven people called up to the Torah on Shabbat. Many people read the portion during the week, a section a day, together with a commentary. The links on this page open the portion on Sefaria, with the Hebrew text beside an English translation, so you can read it before Shabbat or follow along in synagogue.

Questions about the weekly Torah portion

What parsha are we in this week?

The portion for this coming Shabbat is shown at the top of this page, calculated for today's date: its English and Hebrew name, the book and the exact verses, with a link to read it. Outside Israel and in Israel the portion is usually the same; in the weeks when they differ, the page shows both.

What is today's daily Torah portion?

The weekly portion is read in full in the synagogue on Shabbat morning. On Shabbat afternoon and on Monday and Thursday mornings, the first section of the next Shabbat's portion is read. Many people also study the weekly portion a little each day during the week, so that by Shabbat they have read all of it; one way is to read one of its seven sections (aliyot) each day.

What is my Torah portion?

Usually this means the portion of your bar or bat mitzvah, or of the week you were born. Enter a date of birth in the "What is my Torah portion?" calculator on this page: it finds the Hebrew birthday, the bar mitzvah date (13 years) or bat mitzvah date (12 years) on the Hebrew calendar, and the portion read on the first Shabbat on or after it.

What is the Torah portion?

The Torah, the Five Books of Moses, is divided into 54 portions (parshiot; one is a parsha or parashah). One portion is read each Shabbat in order, so the whole Torah is read every year. Genesis has 12 portions, Exodus 11, Leviticus 10, Numbers 10 and Deuteronomy 11.

Why are some weeks a double portion?

There are 54 portions but fewer Shabbatot available in most years, because some Shabbatot fall on holidays that have their own reading. So in some years up to seven pairs are read together: Vayakhel-Pekudei, Tazria-Metzora, Achrei Mot-Kedoshim, Behar-Bechukotai, Chukat-Balak, Matot-Masei, Nitzavim-Vayeilech. In a Jewish leap year, which has an extra month, most of them are read separately. In 5787 there are 3 double-portion weeks.

Why is the Torah portion different in Israel?

Outside Israel, the last day of Pesach and the second day of Shavuot are holidays, and when one of them falls on Shabbat, a holiday reading replaces the weekly portion. In Israel that day is an ordinary Shabbat, so Israel reads the next portion and is one week ahead. A few weeks later the diaspora joins two portions together and the two calendars line up again. In 5787 they differ for 6 weeks.

When does the Torah reading start again?

On Simchat Torah the last portion, V'Zot HaBerachah, is read, and immediately afterward the Torah is started again from the beginning of Genesis. Parashat Bereshit is then read on the following Shabbat. V'Zot HaBerachah is the only portion that is not read on a Shabbat.

What is a haftarah?

The haftarah is a reading from the books of the Prophets that follows the Torah reading on Shabbat and holidays. Each portion has its own haftarah, usually connected to it in theme; on some special Shabbatot a different haftarah is read.

How is the bar mitzvah date calculated?

By the Hebrew calendar: a boy becomes bar mitzvah on his Hebrew birthday when he turns 13, and a girl becomes bat mitzvah on her Hebrew birthday when she turns 12. The Hebrew day begins at nightfall, so a baby born after sunset has the next Hebrew date; tick "born after sunset" in the calculator. If the Hebrew birthday is in Adar or on the 30th of a month, the calculator notes it; ask your rabbi.

Which calendar does this page use?

The fixed Hebrew calendar, calculated in your browser with the open-source hebcal library, for the diaspora (the default here, for the USA) or for Israel. The verse ranges and the links to read each portion come from Sefaria.
