Orthodox Conversion to Judaism - 85 Questions and Answers

85 questions and answers in plain language, arranged by the stages of the path: before the conversion, conversion day, after the conversion and within the family. Each answer relies on a halakha in the book "Peninei Halakha – Conversion", with a link to the full halakha (in Hebrew).

85 questions and answers

Before the conversion

Who examines, what is examined, and what is important to know before starting.

Is a convert of Ammonite or Moabite descent restricted in marriage today?

The book explains that over the many generations that have passed, the Ammonites and Moabites became mixed with other nations, and the prohibition is void. Rabbi Yehoshua explained that Sennacherib, king of Assyria, confused and mixed up all the nations, and therefore many of those who live in the lands of Ammon and Moab are in practice not Ammonites or Moabites, and the prohibition does not apply to them. According to the book, this is the halakhic ruling. When the prohibition applied, it applied to males only, and female converts from Ammon and Moab could also marry Israelites.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 3, halakha 19 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is it possible to convert without a beit din (rabbinical court), and why is one needed?

Conversion must be done before a beit din. The reason is that a change in the status of the convert is a legal act that has implications for all of Israel.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

How many judges must there be in the beit din for a conversion?

A minor Sanhedrin of twenty-three judges is not needed. A beit din of three judges is enough.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What does the beit din examine in the first stage of conversion?

In the first stage the beit din examines the convert's desire to enter the people of Israel and to accept the Torah and the mitzvot, and decides whether to accept him.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

When does the beit din need to be present in the rest of the process, and at what hours does it sit?

In the second stage the beit din must be present at the brit mila for men, and at the immersion for men and women. The book writes that care should be taken that the circumcision and the immersion take place during the day.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Who is responsible for conversion in the community, and why?

It is the custom of Israel that the rabbis of the place are responsible for conversion. The reason is that the acceptance of converts should be with the broad agreement of the whole community.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is the beit din allowed to delay my conversion if I am serious and want to convert?

It is a mitzvah to bring close and convert righteous converts. From the moment it is known that the convert's desire is genuine, the conversion should not be delayed.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What intention is required in conversion, and what does the beit din clarify?

Conversion must be for the sake of Heaven, that is, out of identification with Judaism. Therefore the beit din must clarify why the non-Jew is coming to convert.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What happens if the beit din accepted a person who converted for the sake of marriage?

From the outset one should not accept a convert who converts for the sake of marriage. But if he was accepted, his conversion is a conversion.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Can the beit din know for certain what is in my heart?

No. The beit din cannot know for certain whether the convert is telling the truth, nor how he will behave in the future. The book notes that experience also teaches that it is hard to foresee the future.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

If one beit din refused to accept me, am I allowed to turn to another rabbi?

A convert, too, may choose to convert with rabbis who follow the path of Hillel. If he asked to convert with one rabbi and was not accepted, he may go to another rabbi. And a rabbi is also permitted to accept a convert who was rejected by another rabbi.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 4, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Why does the beit din ask me why I want to convert, and what happens if I decide to give up?

This is what the Sages instructed: the beit din asks the convert why he wants to convert, and reminds him of the great suffering that the people of Israel went through throughout history and of the antisemitism that exists even today. The book explains that someone who wants to rise in righteousness and morality is not obligated to convert, because every person from every nation can be righteous. If one who asked to convert gives up the request, he is parted from with respect.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Will all the mitzvot of the Torah be laid on me at once before the conversion?

The Sages instructed to teach the one who is converting only some of the lighter mitzvot and some of the weightier mitzvot, and not to say too much and not to be exacting. The reason is that if he hears many weighty mitzvot all at once he may recoil and withdraw, out of concern that he will not be able to learn and keep everything. If he learns gradually, in time he will succeed in keeping them.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is it permitted to teach Torah to someone who has not yet converted?

According to the overwhelming majority of the halakhic authorities, it is permitted, and even a mitzvah, to teach Torah to one who comes to convert. There are stringent authorities who hold that he should not be taught Torah until after the conversion.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What is taught first of all to someone who is preparing to convert?

First of all he is taught about the great purpose of the people of Israel: faith in Hashem, which is meant to elevate a person to be good and moral, to do charity and justice, and to bring blessing to the world. He is also taught about the period of slavery in Egypt and about the exodus from Egypt.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Will the study focus only on the mitzvot between man and God?

No. The study toward conversion places a central emphasis on the mitzvot between a person and his fellow, such as "love your neighbor as yourself", charity and helping others. The mitzvot related to the family also receive a central place in the study.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Conversion day

Acceptance of the mitzvot, brit mila, immersion, and what happens afterwards.

Why does a man undergo brit mila before the immersion and not the reverse?

Care is taken to put the brit mila before the immersion, because the circumcision may deter the convert, and therefore it is right to check first that he is indeed ready to undergo it, and only afterwards to complete the conversion with immersion. In addition, according to many, as long as the convert is uncircumcised, the immersion does not help him.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What happens if I already immersed before I was circumcised?

The book brings a dispute among the Rishonim: some hold that after the fact the immersion counted, and some hold that it is not valid while the convert is uncircumcised. In practice, according to the book, one who immersed before the circumcision should immerse again after it, but should not recite a blessing.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Who can circumcise me, and who needs to be present at the brit mila?

The circumcision is done as all of Israel perform brit mila: by a Jewish mohel, male, with the intention of a mitzvah. The book brings that many hold that a Jewish woman is also fit to circumcise, but in practice the custom is that only a Jewish male circumcises. The brit is done during the day and before a beit din of three, and the mohel counts as one of the three. These three do not need to be Torah scholars.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 7 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is the brit painful for an adult, and is anesthesia given?

When the one entering the covenant is an adult, the circumcision is more painful for him, and therefore it is customary to anesthetize the place of the circumcision. The book adds that in practice they usually settle for a local anesthetic.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 7 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

When do I immerse after the brit?

After the convert recovers from the circumcision, he immerses and completes his conversion. Although it is a mitzvah to complete the conversion promptly, the immersion is delayed until he recovers, because there is a concern that the water will contaminate the wound.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 7 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What should the immersion in the mikveh look like?

The immersion is an immersion of the whole body at once in the water of the mikveh. If even one finger or one hair remains outside the water, the immersion is invalid. It also does not help to immerse again only the finger or the hair; rather, one goes back and immerses the whole body with all the hair at once.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

How does one prepare for the immersion, and what counts as a barrier?

There must be no barrier on the body or on the hair, such as glue or paint, that prevents the water from coming into contact with them. Therefore, before the immersion one should wash the whole body well and shampoo the hair, and also clean the teeth of food residue. It is also customary to trim the nails, and women customarily remove rings and earrings.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Can a woman immerse for her conversion during her period?

A woman's conversion immersion is not connected to the impurity of niddah, and therefore, if there is a need to hasten the conversion, a woman can immerse for her conversion during her period. For the sake of the cleanliness of the mikveh, she should first clean the vagina.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Does a married female convert need to count seven clean days before the immersion?

A married female convert does not need to purify herself from the blood she saw before her conversion, because it is not impure by Torah law, and therefore she does not need to count seven clean days before the immersion. The book brings that some halakhic authorities wrote that she must, but since the law is rabbinic and many are lenient, the halakha follows them.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Who is present at the immersion, and what am I asked before it?

The conversion is sealed with the immersion, and from the moment the convert has immersed in the water he is a Jew forever. The immersion must take place before a beit din of three men. Before the immersion they check again that the convert wants to convert.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 9 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What is said to me after I come out of the water?

The judges and those accompanying receive the convert, man or woman, with words of respect and love. They say good and comforting things to him, and the judges, who at first challenged him with questions, tell him that everything they said to him was in order to increase his reward.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 10 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

My spouse and I converted together. Do we need to marry again?

A non-Jewish couple who converted must marry according to the law of Moses and Israel after their conversion, and without this they are not considered married. Under the chuppah all the betrothal and marriage blessings are recited, and at the end of the wedding meal held on the day of the wedding the Seven Blessings are recited.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 11 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I was circumcised before the conversion. Does anything need to be done for me?

A convert whose foreskin was removed while he was a non-Jew, not for the sake of Judaism, must have a drop of covenant blood drawn from him. The mohel makes a slight scratch at the place where the foreskin was attached, in order to bring out a drop of blood, and in this way the convert enters the covenant. The book brings that according to many a blessing must be recited for this, but some hold that there is no obligation to draw blood from one who has no foreskin, and therefore no blessing is recited.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 12 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I had a brit mila as a child because my father is Jewish and my mother is not. Does a drop of covenant blood need to be drawn from me?

One who is the son of a Jewish father and a non-Jewish mother, and had a brit mila done in the Jewish way, and comes to convert as an adult, does not need to have a drop of covenant blood drawn again. The book brings that some hold that it is required, because the brit was not done for the sake of conversion, but the main view is that of the overwhelming majority of the halakhic authorities, who do not require it. If the convert is interested, it is good to draw a drop of covenant blood from him.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 12 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Do I need to bring a sacrifice in order to convert?

When the Temple stood, the convert offered a sacrifice to complete his conversion. When it is not possible to offer sacrifices, the convert converts without a sacrifice, because in all generations it is possible to convert. The book explains that when the Temple is rebuilt the convert will bring his sacrifice.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 5, halakha 14 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

After the conversion

Daily life: prayer, customs, vessels and mitzvot.

Do I need to repent for things I did before the conversion?

The convert's sins are forgiven him, and therefore he does not need to do individual repentance for transgressions he committed when he was a gentile. The book explains that there is no deeper and greater repentance than the choice to join the people of Israel and to accept the Torah and the mitzvot. The sins that are forgiven are sins between man and God; obligations toward people remain.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Does the conversion also erase debts and money that I owed people before the conversion?

The conversion forgives the sins between man and God, but it does not cancel obligations that the convert owed to other people, Jews or non-Jews. Loans he took, he must repay, and damages he caused, he must pay for.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I am not a descendant of the Patriarchs. Do I say in the prayer "our God and God of our fathers"?

Yes. The convert says in all the prayers and blessings like all of Israel: "our God and God of our fathers, God of Abraham, God of Isaac and God of Jacob". The reason is that Abraham our father is the father of Israel and of the converts alike.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

How does one say in the prayer the passages about the exodus from Egypt, such as "that You took us out of the land of Egypt"?

In passages that mention the miracles that were done for Israel in the exodus from Egypt, the convert can choose: to say "that You took Israel out of Egypt", or to say like the text of all of Israel. In practice the custom is always to say like the text of all of Israel, because the convert is included in Israel.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Can I say the morning blessing "who did not make me a gentile", when I was born a non-Jew?

The book brings several opinions: some say that the convert should say "who made me a convert", and some say that he should not recite the blessing at all. But according to the book, in practice one who wants to recite it like all of Israel may do so, because when he converted it became clear that at the root of his soul there was a spark of Israel.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Can a convert be a prayer leader or lead the zimun for Birkat HaMazon?

The book brings an early opinion according to which a convert cannot, but it explains that the halakha does not follow it. According to the Shulchan Aruch as brought in the book, the convert can be a cantor in the regular text and say the regular text of Birkat HaMazon, and therefore he can also lead the zimun. The book adds that the Acharonim also agreed to this.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I have no ancestral custom. According to which prayer text and community custom do I conduct myself?

Every person continues in the customs of his fathers in the text of the prayer and in matters of halakha. A convert who has no ancestral custom can choose the custom that is preferable to him, and he will follow it in all matters of prayer and halakha, because with a convert the aspect of choice is more prominent.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What if I chose a custom and afterwards I want to move to another custom?

One can perform annulment of vows and move to the custom that seems more suitable. The book adds that it is right to do so in consultation with the rabbi, because the change may weaken the convert or the members of his family.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Do I need to immerse in a mikveh the vessels I have at home?

A convert does not need to immerse his eating vessels. The reason is that at the time he immerses and becomes an Israelite, the vessels in his possession also become purified and are elevated together with him.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I used my vessels for forbidden foods before the conversion. What is done with them?

If he used them while still a non-Jew for forbidden foods, he must kasher them according to the rule "as it absorbed, so it expels" (כבולעו כך פולטו). Vessels in which he ate or drank cold foods or drinks – he should clean well with water. Vessels in which he cooked or placed hot foods – he should clean and afterwards purge in boiling water. Vessels in which he baked or roasted foods by the heat of the oven or over fire – he should kasher by heating until glowing, according to the way they were used. The details of the laws are in the book "Peninei Halakha – Kashrut", chapter 33.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Am I forbidden to eat a kosher dish that I cooked myself before the conversion, because of the prohibition of food cooked by non-Jews?

In kosher foods that the convert cooked before his conversion there is no prohibition of food cooked by non-Jews. The prohibition is out of a concern that people will come to assimilate among the gentiles, and there is no such concern with one who prepared the dish, because in the meantime he converted and became a Jew.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I gave birth or miscarried before the conversion. Is the first son born to me after the conversion obligated in redemption?

If the female convert gave birth or miscarried while a non-Jew, the first son she will bear after the conversion is not considered one who first opens the womb, and he is exempt from redemption, because her womb has already been opened. This is despite the saying that a convert who has converted is like a child newly born.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Do I need to separate challah from dough that I started kneading before the conversion?

The time of the obligation of challah is at the time of kneading. If the convert kneaded the dough before he converted and baked it after the conversion, the dough is exempt from challah. And if he kneaded it after the conversion, it is obligated in challah.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I do not know all the details of the laws of prayer and blessings. Must I keep them exactly right after the conversion?

No. The convert needs to get used to keeping the mitzvot gradually, and if he was not accustomed before the conversion to keeping the laws of prayer and blessings in all their fine points, he is not obligated to be pressed to keep them immediately, but should become accustomed to them according to his ability.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

There was a mezuzah on the doorway of the house when I converted. Does it need to be replaced?

If the convert affixed the mezuzah while he was a gentile, he should remove it and affix it again with a blessing. And if it was affixed by a Jew who lived there before and it remained after he left, there is no need to remove it.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 9, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Family

Parents, mourning, inheritance, marriage and more.

Does the law in the state change anything regarding the inheritance of my children?

The book explains that today, as a result of the law, the convert's children or his biological relatives acquire his property even without a will, because his property becomes ownerless and according to the law only they are entitled to use his property. If he has no children or relatives, the law establishes that the state acquires his property.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Am I obligated to honor my non-Jewish parents after the conversion?

It is a mitzvah for the convert to honor his non-Jewish parents, and it is forbidden for him to disgrace them. The mitzvah of honoring parents in its strict definitions does not apply to him, but in everything that natural morality requires, the convert too is obligated toward his parents.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

If I am considered newly born, am I exempt from the obligations I had toward my parents?

The book explains that no. The convert is not exempt from his previous obligations; rather, he has risen to a higher and more sanctified level, and therefore all the obligations that natural morality requires remain upon him. If he violates them after the conversion, this will be a desecration of the Name.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

In practice, how should I behave toward my non-Jewish parents?

A convert is forbidden to disgrace his parents, and all the more so he is forbidden to strike them. It is a mitzvah that he behave toward them with respect, and help them in their illness or old age, as accepted morality requires.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

My parents converted together with me. Am I obligated to honor them like a person born Jewish honors his parents?

Even when parents converted together with their children, the obligation of honoring parents in its legal definitions does not apply to them. But since they brought their children to conversion, there is a moral obligation on the children to honor them at least as all of Israel must honor their parents and their rabbis.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

I am afraid my parents will have a bad influence on my children. What should I do?

It is right for the convert to set his residence far from his parents, and in this way, without hurting them, he distances himself and his children from their influence. If he fears that even during visits they will influence his children for the worse, he should reduce the visits to the extent that he will still not be considered ungrateful toward them.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Am I permitted to pray for my sick parents, who are not Jewish?

When the convert's parents are sick, even if they believe in another religion, it is fitting for their son to pray for their recovery. If they are interested in this, it is fitting that he ask the gabbai to recite a "Mi Sheberach" for their recovery.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

My parents are interested in Judaism. How is it good to act?

If the convert's parents are interested in Judaism, it is good that he bring them closer to the mitzvot of the Torah. If they are happy to take part with him in Shabbat meals, it is good to include them. In this way the concern that the parents will have a bad influence on the grandchildren decreases, and it is possible that they will even encourage the grandchildren to hold fast to Judaism and the mitzvot.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Am I obligated to mourn my non-Jewish parents or relatives when they pass away?

The mitzvah of mourning for first-degree relatives applies only to Jews, and therefore the convert is not obligated to mourn his non-Jewish relatives. But if he wishes, he can mourn them as is customary among them, and if he wishes to sit shiva for them according to the custom of Israel, he is permitted.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Can I say Kaddish for my parents who have passed away?

It is right for the convert to say "Kaddish Yatom" for his parents in the year of their passing, and even if they did not convert, it is fitting that he show them gratitude for having given birth to him. If they converted with him, it is a mitzvah for him to say Kaddish for the elevation of their souls. The book also brings an opinion that it is not fitting to say Kaddish for parents who did not convert, and many opinions that it is fitting to say it for them too.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Do children born to me before the conversion count for the mitzvah of "be fruitful and multiply"?

A convert who fathered children before his conversion has fulfilled the mitzvah of "be fruitful and multiply" through them. The book brings a dispute whether this is true even when the children did not convert, or only if they converted.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 7 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

My brother and I converted. Are we permitted to marry relatives who are forbidden to us?

The prohibition on relatives who were forbidden to the convert within the framework of the seven Noahide mitzvot remains also after the conversion. For example, a brother and sister who converted, since it was forbidden for them to marry as non-Jews, are forbidden to marry also after the conversion.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is there a prohibition of yichud (being alone in a room) between relatives who converted?

The prohibition of yichud does not apply to family members who converted, because the natural relationship between them remains as it was. Just as there is no prohibition of yichud for first-degree Jewish relatives, so too there is no prohibition for converts. The book mentions that some of the Acharonim were stringent, and writes that there is no need to be concerned about their words.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 8, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Spouse and children

The spouse of a Jew, conversion of minors and protest upon growing up. Where the book has a dispute, both opinions are brought.

What is the opinion of most halakhic authorities regarding the conversion of a non-Jewish partner of a Jew?

According to the overwhelming majority of the halakhic authorities, one should make an effort to convert the non-Jewish partner and to marry him off properly to the Jewish partner, in order to save the Jew and his offspring from assimilating among the nations. The book writes that from the outset one does not convert for the sake of marriage, but in a time of pressing need, since after the fact the conversion is valid and if they married they will not divorce, one should convert them and marry them.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 6, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

We already live together or are married in a civil marriage. Does this change anything regarding the conversion?

The book explains that if the couple already lived together, and especially if they already married in a civil marriage, according to many the conversion of the non-Jewish partner is considered "for the sake of Heaven". The reason: they do not need the conversion in order to maintain their marriage, and therefore his wish to convert is not for the sake of marriage.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 6, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What is the opinion of the stringent authorities regarding the conversion of couples who do not intend to live a religious life?

According to the stringent authorities, the conversion depends on a sincere commitment to keep all the mitzvot. Their intention is that the convert sincerely commit to a religious way of life as accepted in one of the religious or Haredi circles. It is written that he does not need to keep everything immediately, because he has not yet learned all the halakhot.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 6, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

We are converting. Is it possible to convert our young children together with us?

A beit din can convert minors, and therefore when the parents come to convert and ask to convert their children with them, the beit din converts them. The conversion depends on the decision of the convert, and a child who has not yet reached the age of mitzvot has no mature opinion with which to decide. But it is for the child's benefit to be of the same religion as his parents, who are responsible for him and take care of him, and therefore the beit din converts the minor with his parents' consent.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 7, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is it possible to convert a child even when the parents themselves are not converting, or when the child is adopted?

The book explains that a beit din can convert a minor even when the parents are not interested in converting themselves but want their child to convert, or when the child himself wants to convert and his parents agree. Accepting Judaism is a benefit for the child, and if the beit din assesses that the conversion will indeed be a benefit for him, it will convert him on the authority of the beit din. So too when Jews want to adopt an abandoned baby and convert him, the beit din may respond to their request.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 7, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

My father is Jewish and my mother is not Jewish and objects to my conversion. Is it possible to convert me?

The book brings here two opinions. Many say that the mother is listened to, because the child is a non-Jew and according to halakha the father is not considered his father. And some say that the father too has authority to bring him to conversion, except that if the mother objects, it is often preferable not to convert him, because as a result of her objection she will insist on educating him to hate Judaism.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 7, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What does it mean that a minor who converted can protest when he grows up?

The conversion of a minor takes place on the authority of the beit din, and therefore it depends on the minor, when he grows up and has an independent opinion, not protesting against the conversion. If when he reaches the age of mitzvot, twelve for a girl and thirteen for a boy, he protests against the conversion, the conversion is annulled. The simple way to protest is to announce, even before he reaches the age of mitzvot, that he does not want to be Jewish, and to remain of this opinion until he reaches the age of mitzvot.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 7, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is a child converted when the parents do not intend to raise him to a religious life?

There is a dispute about this. According to the stringent authorities, one can convert only when the parents intend to raise the minor to keep a religious way of life. And according to the lenient authorities, one can convert him even when they intend to raise him to keep mitzvot as people with a Jewish identity. The book explains that the dispute regarding minors generally matches the dispute regarding the conversion of adults, but there are those who were lenient with adults and stringent with minors, and the reverse.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 7, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Why do some authorities allow converting a minor even if he will not receive a religious education?

The lenient authorities answer the claim that conversion is an obligation and not a privilege by saying that even to be a Jew who does not keep a religious way of life is a great privilege. They rely on the words of the Sages that the fire of Gehinnom has no power over the sinners of Israel, and that even at the time that they are impure, the Divine Presence is with them. It is also written that the lenient authorities see importance in the offspring of the Jew remaining within Israel and not being lost among the gentiles.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 7, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Treatment of the convert

Love of the convert and the prohibition to hurt him.

Is there a special mitzvah to love a convert?

Yes. In addition to the mitzvah "you shall love your neighbor as yourself", which includes all of Israel and among them the converts, the Torah added a special mitzvah to love the convert. Therefore, in loving the convert one fulfills two positive commandments from the Torah.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is love of the convert only a feeling in the heart?

Love is not expressed only in the feelings of the heart but also in speech and in action. One who loves his fellow speaks of his praise, and is considerate of his honor and his money as he is considerate of his own honor and his own money.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Does one who converts have a special merit in the eyes of Hashem?

The book brings the words of the Sages that the merit of the converts is greater than the merit of the righteous who love Hashem, because the converts are at a higher level: Hashem loves them. So it is said in the Torah: "and loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment."

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Why does the Torah command so much about love of the convert?

The book brings a parable of the Sages about a deer that joined the flock of the king, and the king loved it. According to the parable, we must be grateful to the convert who left his family, his father's house and his nation and came to us. Therefore Hashem multiplied commands about love of the convert and warned not to distress him.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is it forbidden to distress a convert only in words, or also in monetary matters?

The prohibition of "ona'ah" (causing distress to another) includes two components: ona'at devarim, not to distress with speech, and ona'at mamon, not to distress by harming money. The Torah added two separate warnings not to distress the convert, and therefore one who distresses a convert in words or in money transgresses three prohibitions.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What things is it forbidden to say to a convert, so as not to hurt him?

The book brings an example from the words of the Sages: if a convert comes to study Torah, it is forbidden to say to him, "A mouth that ate carrion and treifot (torn, non-kosher animals), vermin and creeping things, comes to study Torah that was spoken by the mouth of the Almighty?!" A son of converts is also included in the prohibition, and it is forbidden to say to him, "Remember the deeds of your fathers."

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Do the laws about treatment of a convert also apply to a new immigrant?

Yes. Since new immigrants too have similar difficulties, all the mitzvot that Israel were commanded regarding converts also apply to the absorption of new immigrants.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Why must one honor a convert and be especially careful not to distress him?

The book explains that there are two reasons. First, he should be honored for his decision to leave his family, his people, his land and his mother tongue and to join the people of Israel. Second, he is now alone in an unfamiliar society, and any slight insult may shake his confidence, distress him and sadden him.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What can be done so that a convert will be less offended, even when there is no intention to hurt him?

Even when the surroundings make an effort, sometimes people will stumble unintentionally, and sometimes the convert himself will mistakenly think that he was slighted because he does not understand the language and the customs. To reduce these cases, the book writes that one should take care to behave toward him always with great respect, so that he feels loved and accepted, and this will help him overcome the misunderstandings.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 3 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is it good to invite converts to celebrations, such as a brit, a bar mitzvah and a wedding?

Yes. When a person has a celebration and prepares the list of invitees, it is a mitzvah that he remember the converts and the new immigrants and invite them respectfully to his celebration. In this he gives them a feeling of belonging and security, and makes them happy.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 1, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Converts in the history of Israel

Great converts who came before us.

Who was the first convert, and what can be learned from him?

The first of the converts was Abraham our father. He was born into a family of idol worshippers, and through his good character traits and deep reflection he came to know his Creator. The book explains that his life is an example and a model for all converts, who leave their native land and their family, join the people of Israel and take part in bringing blessing to the whole world.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 1 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What motivated Yitro to ask to join the people of Israel?

The book relates that Yitro searched all his life for the purpose of man and the secret of life. When he heard that Hashem had saved Israel from Egypt through Moshe his son-in-law, the desire to draw near to Hashem was awakened in his heart again. According to the book, although it was not easy for a wise and experienced elder to begin to learn a new religion, his longing for the true faith prevailed.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 2 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Can a person who lived a life far from the Torah convert and rise? What does the book learn from Rachav?

Rachav the harlot in Jericho understood that Hashem, God of Israel, rules over everything and is the source of life, and she had the courage to ask to join Israel and to convert. The book concludes that even one who sank into desires and sinned much can believe in Hashem, return in repentance and rise without limit.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 4 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What motivated Ruth to convert, even when she knew that she might remain alone and poor?

Ruth acted out of a moral duty to the memory of her dead husband and out of a desire to stand beside her mother-in-law Naomi, so that she would not remain bereaved and alone. Out of this her heart opened to faith in Hashem, God of Israel, and she decided to convert and to join Israel in an eternal covenant, even if she would remain alone and poor until the day of her death.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

What was the place of Ruth the convert in the people of Israel afterwards?

The book relates that Ruth merited to see David, her great-grandson, save Israel and establish his royal dynasty in Jerusalem. According to the book, everyone knew that the royal family in Israel arose in the merit of the great kindness of the great-grandmother, this righteous convert.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 5 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Did converts also contribute to the study of the Oral Torah? Who were Shemaya and Avtalyon?

Yes. The contribution of the righteous converts to the revelation of the Oral Torah was immense. Shemaya and Avtalyon were among the greatest of the converts, and were descendants of Sennacherib, king of Assyria. The book relates that Shemaya was the Nasi of the Sanhedrin and Avtalyon was the Av Beit Din, and that Hillel was one of their great students.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 6 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)

Is it true that one of the greatest of the Sages of Israel was the son of converts?

Yes. Rabbi Akiva, who studied Torah with immense devotion until he became the greatest of the Sages of the Oral Torah, was the son of converts. The book mentions that he was born to Yosef the convert, and only at the age of forty began to study Torah.

Source: Peninei Halakha – Conversion, chapter 2, halakha 8 · Full halakha on the Peninei Halakha site (in Hebrew)
